# Four Ways to Scale a Service Business

> Framework from [[Alex Hormozi]] — the only four vehicles to package a service business for scale, each with different trade-offs on cash flow, enterprise value, and capital requirements.

## The Four Models

### 1. Private Chain
You own every location end-to-end. You take all the risk, front all the capital, hire all the labor, control the brand — but you own 100% of the revenue.

**When it makes sense:**
- Low build-out cost (less than 6 months of first-year profit per location)
- Low service requirements (minimized variability, productizable labor)
- Example: Insomnia Cookies — tiny locations, flour/sugar margins, massive profit vs. cost

**Pros:** Highest cash flow, highest enterprise value (you own everything)
**Cons:** Highest capital cost per location, all risk is yours

### 2. Franchise
You sell the model to outside investors. They own the locations; you collect royalties off top-line revenue.

**When it makes sense:**
- Very high build-out costs per location (e.g., McDonald's = ~$1M per location)
- You want to crowdsource the capital risk
- You can productize the service to near-zero variability

**Pros:** High enterprise value, other people front the capital
**Cons:** Very expensive to start (~$750K in legal/setup), 1-2 years to profit, very litigious, you only earn royalties (small % of top line)

**Rule of thumb:** Need 100+ locations to make real money. 90-95% of franchises never hit 100.

### 3. Licensing
You license 2 of 3 franchise components (name, business systems, fee) — never all three (that's an illegal franchise). Examples: CrossFit (name + fee, no systems), white-label models (systems + fee, no brand).

**When it makes sense:**
- Low or no capital
- Need cash flow day one
- IP is offer-driven rather than protectable (easy to copy = go fast)
- Example: Gym Launch — offer-driven, needed speed, no capital

**Pros:** Almost no cost to start, super high margins, day-one cash flow, fastest market capture
**Cons:** Not defensible, low enterprise value, low exit multiples (need 80%+ yearly retention for a 6x+ exit)

### 4. Software / Tech-Enabled Service
Replace or augment the service with software.

**When it makes sense:**
- The service can be replaced by software (not haircuts)
- You have a technical co-founder who can code (do NOT outsource — Hormozi lost $3M learning this)
- You can tolerate years of low cash flow for a high exit

**Pros:** Highest enterprise value on exit
**Cons:** Tons of cost and time to build, very low cash flow during build, same 80%+ yearly retention bar to be valued as software (not just a service with tech)

## The Decision Matrix

| Factor | Private Chain | Franchise | Licensing | Software |
|--------|--------------|-----------|-----------|----------|
| **Capital cost** | High (per location) | High (~$750K startup) | Near zero | High (dev cost) |
| **Cash flow** | Highest | Slow (royalties only) | Day one, high | Very low during build |
| **Enterprise value** | High | High | Low (unless 80%+ retention) | Highest |
| **Speed to market** | Slow (per location) | Slow (1-2 yrs to profit) | Fastest | Slowest |
| **Defensibility** | High (own everything) | Medium | Low | High (if retention holds) |
| **Risk** | All yours | Shared with franchisees | Low | High (build cost + retention risk) |

## Key Insight: Cash Flow vs. Exit Value

There's a spectrum: **Licensing** makes money throughout the process but sacrifices exit value. **Software** sacrifices cash flow during build for the highest exit. Private chain and franchise sit in between.

## Relevance to Exo

This framework directly applies to how Exo packages its AI department offering:
- **Licensing model** = the Exo-Vaults client delivery system (vault templates licensed per client)
- **Software model** = Flow OS (the productized platform)
- **Private chain** = running Exo's own operations (Client Zero)

Exo's path: start with licensing (vaults, low capital, fast cash flow), build toward software (Flow OS, high enterprise value). The 80%+ yearly retention bar is the metric to watch.
